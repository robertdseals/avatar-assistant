Protecting your plants from a Central Florida freeze

Zones 9a and 9b are warm, but they are not freeze-proof. University of Florida IFAS says freezing weather normally occurs in north and central Florida. That includes Ocala, Marion County, and The Villages. At the Ocala station, NOAA’s 1991–2020 climate normals put the median first fall freeze, a low of 32°F, on December 12, and the median last spring freeze on February 25. About one year in ten, the first freeze comes by November 21, or the last freeze comes after March 21. Check the forecast. A calm night can still hurt a tomato, a tropical, or a new planting. This page is the home version of the UF/IFAS guide Cold Protection of Landscape Plants. It is general guidance, not a visit from the Extension office.

Two kinds of cold nights

A radiational freeze is a calm, clear night. Heat leaves the plants and the soil. Moist air may leave frost. Dry air may leave no ice, and the plants can still be hurt. A cover that reaches the ground traps some of that ground heat. An advective freeze is a cold wind from the north. The drop is sudden, and the wind carries heat away. UF/IFAS says those windy nights are harder to protect against. A fence, a house wall, or a hedge can slow a short blast of wind. A windbreak does little on a still, clear frost.

What is most likely to be hurt

Tropical plants and summer annuals do not harden for a freeze. Many suffer chilling injury below 50°F, well before 32. Subtropical plants can adjust if the cooling is gradual. The same low temperature usually does more damage in late fall than in January or February, because the plants have not settled. Roots in a small pot freeze more easily than roots in the ground. Cold air settles in low spots. Mix tender plants with hardier ones so one night does not erase the yard.

Tomatoes, peppers, and other warm-season vegetables are damaged or killed by frost. The Florida Vegetable Gardening Guide says frosts happen occasionally in Central Florida. See the planting calendar and the fall vegetable guide.

Before the night arrives

Do not prune in late summer or early fall, and do not fertilize late in the fall. The tender flush that follows is the first tissue to burn. A healthy plant handles cold better than one worn down by insects or disease. Follow the product label rather than guessing a spray mix. On a calm night, a tree canopy holds some heat and can reduce bark splitting when morning sun hits frozen wood. Plants that like a little shade also dry out less than plants in full sun.

The day a freeze is forecast

Water if the soil is dry. Moist soil stores more sun than dry soil and gives a little heat back at night. Do not leave the bed soggy. Turn off in-ground sprinklers. Farmers sometimes sprinkle all night because freezing water can hold a leaf near 32°F, but a home system cannot keep that film of water going. Ice breaks branches, the soil floods, and many watering rules do not allow it. Bring pots inside, or push them together and mulch them, then space them out again after the cold. Mulch perennial roots. For many perennials, the crown is what must live. The top may die back and sprout again.

Covers help more against frost than against extreme cold. Use frost cloth, canvas, a sheet, or a quilt. It must reach the ground and be weighted if the wind is up, and it should not rest on the leaves. A box or a weighted jug can cover a small plant. Plastic sheeting belongs on a frame, not on the foliage. Take covers off once the temperature is above freezing. On a sunny day, remove or vent plastic so heat does not build up. Plastic touching a leaf pulls heat away and can worsen the damage. An outdoor-approved incandescent bulb, or outdoor incandescent holiday lights, can add a little heat under a cover. LED holiday lights stay cool and do not add meaningful heat. Keep any bulb off the cloth and the leaves. If a light is not rated for the outdoors, leave it inside.

The morning after

A windy freeze dries leaves while cold roots cannot keep up. Water so the plant can drink, a frozen pot can thaw, and leftover fertilizer salts are diluted. Wait to prune cold-damaged plants until the risk of another freeze has passed. UF/IFAS Extension Lake County puts that around mid-March for much of central Florida. At the Ocala station, NOAA still shows a 32°F night after March 21 in about one year in ten, so do not cut while a freeze is in the forecast. Live wood is green just under the bark. Dead wood is brown or black. Damaged leaves still insulate the living parts. Cover new shoots if another freeze is coming. For grafted gardenias and many fruit trees, UF/IFAS describes wrapping the trunk, or mounding soil one to two feet up it, to save the graft. Remove that wrap or mound in spring.
