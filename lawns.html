Best lawn grasses for sandy Ocala soil

Ocala, Marion County, and The Villages sit on sandy ground in zones 9a and 9b. University of Florida IFAS says central Florida soil is often acidic sand, and in places muck. Sand does not hold water or fertilizer for long. Get a soil test from the Marion County Extension office before you buy sod. These notes come from Selecting a Turfgrass for Florida Lawns and the species guides below.

Skip fescue, bluegrass, and ryegrass as a permanent lawn. They can grow in the cool months and will not survive a hot, humid Florida summer.

St. Augustinegrass

St. Augustinegrass is the most common lawn grass in Florida. It handles sand or heavier soil and a pH from acidic to mildly alkaline. Shade tolerance is good to excellent by variety. Drought tolerance is good, and wear tolerance is poor. Plant sod, plugs, or sprigs. There is no seed. In central and north Florida, plant during active growth, not in a cold winter or a hot, dry summer. Mow standard types at 3.5 to 4 inches and dwarfs at about 2 to 2.5 inches. Never remove more than one-third of the leaf at once.

Floratam is the most widely used St. Augustinegrass in Florida. Its cold tolerance is poor next to other St. Augustine varieties. Its shade tolerance is poor too. It does not persist well with less than six hours of sun. Bitterblue has good cold and shade tolerance statewide. CitraBlue, from the University of Florida, is more drought tolerant than Floratam, Palmetto, and Raleigh. The southern chinch bug is the main insect. Follow the product label. This page does not give spray rates.

Bahiagrass

Bahiagrass roots deeply in infertile, acidic sand and gets by with less water and fertilizer. UF/IFAS recommends it where there is no irrigation. In a long drought it turns brown, rests, and greens up after rain. Do not mow it short or fertilize it while it is dormant. Mow at 3 to 4 inches. Drought tolerance is excellent, salt tolerance very poor, shade fair, and wear poor.

‘Argentine’ is the home-lawn type: darker, denser, and cold tolerant. ‘Pensacola’ makes more seed heads. ‘Common’ is not recommended. Use seed or sod. In the Bahia guide, north Florida is anything north of Ocala, and central Florida runs south from Ocala toward Vero Beach and Tampa. Both regions are listed at 1 to 2 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet per year. Feed first in early April south of Ocala, and mid-April north of town.

Zoysiagrass

Zoysiagrass grows statewide on a wide range of soils, with excellent drought tolerance. In a dry week it turns brown and rests, then greens up when water returns. ‘Empire’ is the most common zoysiagrass in Florida. It has done well in sand and in clay, and it is susceptible to large patch. Mow medium and coarse types when they reach 3 to 4 inches, down to 1.75 to 2.5 inches. Extra nitrogen builds thatch. Large patch can start in fall when the soil about 4 inches down is 65°F to 75°F. Seed from April through July. North of Ocala, fall seeding is a poor idea because the seedlings may not be ready for winter cold.

Centipedegrass

Centipedegrass likes acidic, infertile soil and has fair shade tolerance. Its guide says it is well adapted to Central and Northern Florida at a pH of about 4.5 to 6.5, and that iron chlorosis can show up above a pH of about 6.5 to 7. The statewide selection table lists it only for North Florida and the Panhandle, at a mowing height of 1.5 to 2 inches, and says centipede and Bahia can run short of micronutrients above a pH of about 8. The two pages do not give the same map or the same pH line. Let a soil test decide. Inside a pH of about 4.5 to 6.5, the centipede guide treats it as a central and north Florida grass. On alkaline ground, choose another grass.

Water, fertilizer, and a lawn you do not mow

Leave the clippings. At the right height they feed the sand and do not make thatch. For St. Augustinegrass and bahiagrass, water when blades fold, turn blue-gray, or hold your footprints, about one-half to three-quarters of an inch.

In unincorporated Marion County, Land Development Code section 6.8.14 sets the fertilizer limits. One application may not exceed 0.7 pounds of readily available nitrogen or 1 pound of total nitrogen per 1,000 square feet. Yearly nitrogen ceilings, in pounds per 1,000 square feet, are 3 for Bahia and St. Augustine, 2 for centipede, and 4 for zoysia and Bermuda. Do not apply nitrogen or phosphorus before seeding or sodding, or for the first 30 days after. On existing turf and landscape, phosphorus is capped at 0.25 pounds per 1,000 square feet per application and 0.50 pounds per year. Follow the bag. Most Marion soils already have plenty of phosphorus, so test before you add any. Use the lower of the county ceiling and the UF/IFAS range for your side of Ocala. Bahia stays at 1 to 2 pounds a year. North of Ocala, keep centipede inside 0.4 to 2 pounds and zoysia inside 2 to 3. Stop at the county ceiling where a guide goes higher: St. Augustinegrass is listed up to 5 pounds in central Florida and 4 north of Ocala, and central Florida centipede up to 3.

Keep fertilizer off pavement. The code keeps it 30 feet from a pond, stream, watercourse, lake, canal, or wetland. It keeps turf fertilizer 75 feet from the water-body setback line of a spring, pond, stream, watercourse, lake, wetland, sinkhole, or other open karst feature, and 15 feet from the top of a drainage-retention bank, canal, or seawall. With a deflector shield, a drop spreader, or a sharp-edged liquid applicator, that bank distance is 3 feet. The county Be wise when you fertilize page says 100 feet from the edge of a sinkhole and 75 feet from the ordinary high-water line of a natural water body. Where the page and the code differ, use the farther distance.

Heavy shade is a poor home for turf. Sunshine mimosa, zones 8a to 11, takes light traffic in full sun and can spread. Perennial peanut, zones 8b to 11, blooms gold, needs little fertilizer, and does not want foot traffic. Frogfruit, zones 8a to 11, takes light traffic and may go dormant in winter.
