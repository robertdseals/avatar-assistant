Best lawn grasses for sandy Ocala soil

Ocala, Marion County, and The Villages sit on sandy ground in zones 9a and 9b. University of Florida IFAS says central Florida soil is often more acidic than the coast, and it may be sand or, in places, muck. Sand does not hold water or fertilizer for long. Get a soil test from the Marion County Extension office before you buy sod. The notes below come from Selecting a Turfgrass for Florida Lawns and the species guides at the bottom.

Skip northern lawn grasses as a permanent lawn. Fescue, bluegrass, and ryegrass can grow here in fall, winter, and early spring. They will not survive a hot, humid Florida summer.

St. Augustinegrass

St. Augustinegrass is the most common lawn grass in Florida. It handles sand or heavier soil and a pH from acidic to mildly alkaline. Shade tolerance is good to excellent by variety, and salt tolerance is relatively good. Drought tolerance is rated good, not excellent, so it still needs water in a long dry spell. Wear tolerance is poor. Plant sod, plugs, or sprigs. There is no seed. In central and north Florida, plant during active growth and avoid a cold winter or a hot, dry summer. Mow standard types at 3.5 to 4 inches and dwarfs at about 2 to 2.5 inches. Never remove more than one-third of the leaf at once.

Floratam is the most widely used, but its cold and shade tolerance are poor next to other St. Augustine varieties. It does not persist well with less than six hours of sun, which matters where Ocala gets a freeze. Bitterblue has good cold and shade tolerance statewide. CitraBlue, from the University of Florida, is more drought tolerant than Floratam, Palmetto, and Raleigh. The southern chinch bug is the main insect. Follow the product label. This page does not give spray rates.

Bahiagrass

Bahiagrass was a pasture grass for sandy southeastern soils. It roots deeply and hangs on in infertile, acidic sand with less water and fertilizer. UF/IFAS recommends it for a large lot or a yard with no irrigation. It will not look like a carpet. In a long drought it turns brown, rests, and greens up after rain. Leave it alone if you have no sprinkler. Do not mow it short or fertilize it while it is dormant. Mow at 3 to 4 inches with a sharp blade. The chart rates drought excellent, salt very poor, shade fair, wear poor, and nematodes excellent. Maintenance is low.

‘Argentine’ is the home-lawn type: darker, denser, and cold tolerant. ‘Pensacola’ makes more seed heads. ‘Common’ is not recommended and takes cold poorly. Use seed or sod. Seed is cheaper and slower. Plugs leave bare soil for weeds. In that guide, north Florida is anything north of Ocala, and central Florida runs south from Ocala toward Vero Beach and Tampa. Both are listed at 1 to 2 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet per year. Feed first in early April south of Ocala, and mid-April north of town. Ask Extension which date fits your street.

Zoysiagrass

Zoysiagrass grows statewide on a wide range of soils, with excellent drought tolerance. ‘Empire’ is the most common zoysiagrass in Florida and has done well in sand. Mow medium and coarse types at 1.75 to 2.5 inches. Extra nitrogen builds thatch. Large patch can start in fall when the soil about 4 inches down is 65°F to 75°F. Seed from April through July. North of Ocala, fall seeding is a poor idea.

Centipedegrass

Centipedegrass likes acidic, infertile soil. Its guide says it is well adapted to Central and Northern Florida at a pH of about 4.5 to 6.5, with fair shade tolerance. The statewide selection table instead lists it for North Florida and the Panhandle and a mowing height of 1.5 to 2 inches. The maps do not match. On acidic sand north of Ocala it can be a candidate. Near The Villages, or above a pH of about 8, choose another grass. High pH can leave centipede and Bahia short of micronutrients.

Water, fertilizer, and a lawn you do not mow

Leave the clippings. At the right height they feed the sand and do not make thatch. For St. Augustinegrass and bahiagrass, water when blades fold, turn blue-gray, or hold your footprints, about one-half to three-quarters of an inch. The state turf rule caps a spring or summer application at 2 pounds of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet, and only if at least 65 percent is controlled-release. Quick-release nitrogen is capped at 0.7 pounds at one time. Those are ceilings. Follow the bag and any Marion County rule.

Florida-Friendly Landscaping means the right plant in the right place. Heavy shade is a poor home for turf, so use mulch or a groundcover. Sunshine mimosa is a native for full sun in zones 8a to 11. It has pink flowers, takes light traffic, and can spread next door. Perennial peanut blooms gold into fall, holds sand, needs little fertilizer, and is hardy in zones 8b to 11. It does not want foot traffic. Frogfruit, in zones 8a to 11, takes light traffic and may look dormant in winter, then return. Perennial peanut and sunshine mimosa needed less water in drought than turf alone, according to the UF/IFAS alternatives page.
