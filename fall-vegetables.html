Fall and winter vegetables for Marion County

Fall is the kindest vegetable season in Marion County. The summer heat has eased, and cool crops get a real chance. Ocala, the rest of the county, and The Villages are in zones 9a and 9b. UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions draws Central Florida between State Road 40 and State Road 70. State Road 40 runs through Ocala, so a garden north of that road is on their North Florida list. The Villages sits south of Ocala and fits the Central list. Near the line, read both lists, or ask the Marion County Extension office. The Central Florida names are also on our planting calendar.

Two waves, not one planting day

The Florida Vegetable Gardening Guide says vegetables can grow year-round if the dates are right. Tomatoes, peppers, and other warm-season crops are damaged or killed by frost, and they dislike cold soil. Frosts happen occasionally in Central Florida. A Hillsborough County Extension note for Central Florida uses about September 15 for warm-season vegetables and about October 15 for cool-season vegetables. North Florida is often about two weeks earlier. Watch the forecast, and use the freeze guide if a cold night follows a planting.

For that September date, start tomato, pepper, and eggplant seeds about six weeks ahead, out of a washing rain. About four weeks ahead, start arugula, bok choy, broccoli, lettuce, kale, collards, and Swiss chard. Cucumbers, zucchini, and yellow squash can be started in pots about ten days ahead, or sown in the garden. Around September 15, direct-sow arugula, bush beans, pole beans, cucumbers, yellow squash, zucchini, and radishes, and set out transplants that already have true leaves. Keep a few extra seedlings. Pests and heavy rain take some of the first planting.

What the Central Florida calendar says for fall

The September Central Florida list on Gardening Solutions is the bridge between summer and the cool garden. Easy transplants that month include Chinese cabbage, collards, eggplant, endive, kale, lettuce, peppers, strawberry, sugarcane, tomatoes, and tropical spinaches. Mustard, pineapple, and spinach are transplanted with care. Seeds that month include bush, lima, and pole beans, carrots, cucumbers, bunching onions, radish, squashes, and turnips. Tomatoes and peppers can still go in, but they are the plants a later freeze will threaten. Have a cover ready.

October is the big cool-season month. Easy transplants include arugula, beets, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, Chinese cabbage, collards, endive, kale, kohlrabi, lettuce, strawberry, sugarcane, Swiss chard, and tropical spinaches. Celery, mustard, pineapple, and spinach need extra care. Seeds are carrots, bulbing and bunching onions, radish, and turnips. On or after about October 15, the Hillsborough note also says to sow beets, radishes, green beans, spinach, and mustard, and to set out the cool-season transplants. It names sweet peas in that sowing list. Sweet peas are an ornamental, not a vegetable. The vegetable peas in the Florida guide are English peas and snow peas, including snap types such as Sugar Snap. For Central Florida, that guide lists them from November through February, not in mid-October. Carrots are sown where they will grow. If summer plants are still cropping, hold the new transplants in larger pots until a space opens.

Green beans are a warm-season crop. The vegetable guide’s Central Florida dates are February through April and August through September. A sowing after about October 15 in Ocala can still be growing when the first freeze arrives. At the Ocala station, NOAA’s median first 32°F night is December 12, and about one year in ten it comes by November 21. Warm-season vegetables are damaged or killed by frost, so cover a late bean row or skip it. A short row of radishes every couple of weeks still gives more than one harvest.

November and December

By November the Central Florida list has settled into cool crops. Easy transplants are arugula, beets, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, Chinese cabbage, collards, endive, kale, kohlrabi, lettuce, sugarcane, and Swiss chard. Transplant with care: celery, mustard, pineapple, potatoes, and spinach. From seed: carrots, bulbing and bunching onions, English peas, radish, and turnips. December uses that same Central Florida list. These are the crops to lean on through winter. They are not a second chance for a big tomato planting. If a tomato is still in the ground from September, protect it on a frost night or pick what is ready.

Southern peas are a warm-season crop on the earlier lists. Read the packet. The words “pea” and “bean” cover more than one plant.

Herbs and a few cautions

UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions publishes a fall herb list rather than a separate month for each herb. The fall herbs are basil, chives, coriander, dill, fennel, garlic, lavender, lemon balm, marjoram, oregano, parsley, sage, tarragon, and thyme. Cilantro is the leaf of the coriander plant. Flowers for fall and winter are on the planting calendar.

Sandy beds dry fast. Rotate plant families and follow the dates. August and September plantings face more heat-loving insects and diseases. Cool-tender vegetables set out late in winter need covers. Squash-family covers come off at bloom because those flowers need bees. This page does not give pesticide amounts. Follow the label, and use Table 1 in the vegetable guide for a crop’s North, Central, or South Florida dates.
