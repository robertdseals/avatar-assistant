What to plant, month by month

These lists are the Central Florida sections of the University of Florida IFAS Gardening Solutions calendars. UF/IFAS calls Central Florida the land between State Road 40 and State Road 70. That covers much of Marion County and The Villages. Gardens north of State Road 40, including parts of Ocala, should also check the North Florida lists on the same pages.

January and February on this Central Florida list include warm-season crops such as tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, squash, and melons. At the Ocala station, NOAA’s 1991–2020 normals put the median last spring freeze, a low of 32°F, on February 25. About one year in ten, the last freeze comes after March 21. The Florida Vegetable Gardening Guide says cold-tender vegetables planted late in winter can be hurt by frost unless they are covered. In Ocala, wait until the forecast is clear of a freeze, often late February and sometimes into March, or use a frost cover. Mid-to-late February is the median, not a promise that frost is finished.

Herbs

UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions publishes a fall herb list rather than a separate herb for every month. Plant these in fall: basil, chives, coriander, dill, fennel, garlic, lavender, lemon balm, marjoram, oregano, parsley, sage, tarragon, and thyme. The list is on Herbs for Fall.

A Hillsborough County Extension note for Central Florida also says marigolds, zinnias, cilantro, dill, and basil can be started about four weeks before a mid-September planting. See Fall Vegetable Planting Dates for Central Florida.

Freeze and hurricane tips

A longer freeze guide is on Protecting your plants from a Central Florida freeze.

When a freeze is coming

Central Florida is subtropical, and a freeze can still happen. Tropical plants, warm-season annuals, and warm-season vegetables are the ones most likely to be hurt. These steps are from the UF/IFAS Extension Lake County cold-protection notes.

Water if the soil is dry. Moist soil holds heat better than dry soil, but do not leave it soggy. Group pots together or bring them indoors. Cover plants with fabric, such as a sheet or blanket. Skip plastic on the leaves. Hold the cover all the way to the ground with bricks or stakes. A frame is better when you can make one, because leaves that touch the cover can still freeze. Take the cover off once the temperature is above freezing. On a warm or sunny day, vent the cover so heat does not build up underneath. LED holiday lights stay cool and do not add meaningful heat. The longer steps are on the freeze guide.

Sprinkling plants with a hose during a freeze is a grower practice. It is not a good idea for a home yard, because the water has to run the whole time and most home sprinklers cannot do that. After the freeze, give plants a thorough drink. Wait to prune cold-damaged growth until the risk of another freeze has passed. UF/IFAS Extension Lake County puts that around mid-March for much of central Florida. At the Ocala station, a 32°F night still comes after March 21 in about one year in ten, so do not cut while a freeze is in the forecast. Damaged leaves left in place can shield the plant on the next cold night.

Source: Cold Protection Frequently Asked Questions.

Before and after a hurricane

Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30. These steps are from UF/IFAS Gardening Solutions.

Plant larger trees away from the house, power lines, and other structures. Sabal palm, crape myrtle, and live oak are among the trees UF/IFAS names for wind resistance. No tree is storm-proof. Ask the Marion County Extension office before adding a tree, and use the UF/IFAS wind-resistance lists. Have a certified arborist look at large trees and remove dead, damaged, or weakly attached limbs before the season. Trees planted in groups can buffer one another.

When a storm is on the way, move pots, hanging baskets, and loose yard objects into a sheltered place. Turn off the irrigation. If a rain barrel is empty, move it. If it is connected to a gutter, disconnect it so it can overflow from the top. If it already holds a lot of water, fill it the rest of the way so it is less likely to blow away.

After the storm, stay away from downed power lines and call the utility company. Do not take on hanging limbs or a leaning tree yourself. Trim only small broken branches you can reach safely.

Sources: Hurricane Landscaping and Preparing Trees for Hurricanes.
