# December in your Central Florida garden

Draft only. Do not send this note, and do not publish it on the website.

Hello from Shaley Gardens. December in Ocala, Marion County, and The Villages is cool-season prime time. The days are short, the bugs are fewer, and the greens are happy. It is also our first real freeze month. At the Ocala station, NOAA's 1991–2020 normals put the median first 32°F freeze around December 12, and about one year in ten it comes by November 21. So if you haven't had a frost yet, assume one is on the way. The median last freeze is February 25, and about one year in ten it comes as late as March 21.

## What to plant now

The UF/IFAS Florida Vegetable Gardening Guide (SP103/VH021) lists the same Central Florida crops for December as for November. Transplants that do well now: beets, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, Chinese cabbage, collards, kale, kohlrabi, lettuce, and Swiss chard. Sow seed of carrots, English peas, bulbing and bunching onions, radish, and turnips. Mustard, spinach, and Irish potatoes can go in too, with a little extra care.

Also good now: arugula, endive/escarole, and celery (celery is slow, at 3+ months, and needs steady moisture). Lettuce, mustard, and Swiss chard can be damaged by a hard freeze, so cover them on cold nights. Use certified seed potatoes, not store-bought.

The UF/IFAS [Central Florida Gardening Calendar](https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP450) suggests cool-weather herbs like parsley, thyme, sage, rosemary, and cilantro, and masses of petunias, pansies, and snapdragons for winter color.

Hold off on tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, and squash. They are frost tender and do better planted in late winter. North of State Road 40, also check the North Florida list.

## Cold protection

Keep frost cloth or old sheets and a few clothespins near the door. When a freeze is forecast:

- Water dry soil the day before so it can hold a little heat, but don't leave it soggy.
- Drape covers all the way to the ground, and keep them off the leaves with stakes if you can. Never lay plastic right on foliage.
- Give extra care to bananas, young citrus, avocado, and papaya. Cover them, or wrap young trunks.
- On a low-pressure well, hand-water the day before, not the night of, so you aren't out with a hose in the cold and the well isn't running at the last minute.
- Move potted plants into the garage or against the house, and group them together.
- Take covers off once it warms above freezing, and vent them on sunny days.

After a cold night, be patient. Wait to prune damaged growth until the danger of frost has passed, late February or early March, once new growth shows. The brown leaves help protect the green wood underneath. More steps are in [Protecting your plants from a Central Florida freeze](https://shaleygardens.com/freeze.html).

## December maintenance

- **Water less.** Cool weather means plants drink less. Check the soil before you water, and follow your local watering days.
- **Mulch.** Two to three inches of pine straw, leaves, or wood chips keeps roots warmer and weeds down. Keep it a few inches back from stems ([UF/IFAS EP625](https://edis.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/EP625)).
- **Skip lawn fertilizer.** UF/IFAS recommends waiting until spring for the first feeding (in Marion County, not before mid-April). Marion County rules don't allow nitrogen or phosphorus fertilizer on saturated soil. Mow less often and leave the clippings.
- **Rake leaves into the compost or beds.** Fallen oak leaves make great free mulch.
- **Clean and sharpen tools** on a rainy day, and jot down what grew well this fall.

## A strong El Niño winter

NOAA's Climate Prediction Center said on October 8 that El Niño is still strengthening, and a strong to very strong event is likely through early 2027. It may even become the strongest on record since 1950. Strong El Niño winters usually bring Florida more rain and more storms, and the general Florida El Niño pattern includes a higher chance of severe weather and tornadoes. NOAA currently favors [near-normal temperatures for our area](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/fxus05.html), so freezes can still happen, especially in January and February.

What that means in the yard:

- **Drainage first.** Raise beds where water stands, and hold off watering after a good rain.
- **Watch for fungus.** Damp weeks bring leaf spots, mildew, and brown patch in St. Augustinegrass. Give plants room for air to move.
- **Keep frost cloth handy.** Cold nights often follow a storm front.
- **Never fertilize before heavy rain.** Runoff can reach our springs and aquifer.
- **Have a storm plan.** Make sure you can get severe-weather warnings, even at night.

See the [CPC ENSO discussion](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/ensodisc.shtml) and the [CPC seasonal outlook](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/seasonal.php?lead=1).
