## A strong El Niño winter: what it may mean for your garden

_Draft only. Reviewed by Fern for Ocala accuracy, Oct 25, 2026._

You may have heard this called a "super" El Niño. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center doesn't use that word, but its October update says El Niño is strengthening and is likely to be strong to very strong through early 2027, with good odds it becomes the strongest since 1950. In north-central Florida, strong El Niño winters usually bring more rain and more frequent storms, including a higher risk of severe weather and tornadoes. Cloudier days tend to run cooler, but NOAA currently favors near-normal temperatures overall, and freezes can still happen, especially in January and February.

What that means in the yard:

- **Drainage first.** Soggy soil invites root rot. Raise beds, keep mulch a few inches off stems, hold off on watering after a good rain, and expect puddles in low spots.
- **Watch for fungus.** Damp, cloudy weeks bring leaf spots, mildew, and brown patch in St. Augustinegrass. Space plants for airflow and water in the morning only.
- **Keep frost cloth handy.** Cold nights often follow a storm front, so cover tender plants once it passes.
- **Plant cool-season crops on time.** Lettuce, kale, broccoli, and other greens love winter. Tomatoes and peppers can wait until spring.
- **Skip lawn fertilizer this winter.** UF/IFAS advises fertilizing Marion County lawns only between April 15 and October 1. County code bans nitrogen or phosphorus fertilizer on saturated soil, and the county and UF/IFAS both advise never fertilizing before heavy rain, since runoff can reach our springs and aquifer.
- **Have a storm plan.** Make sure you have a way to get severe-weather warnings, even at night.
